## **1. Introduction**

The TSEC/KW-26 cryptographic equipment was the first wide-scale, electronic encryption system used by the United States and its military intelligence. It was developed with the National Security Agency (NSA), with the purpose of ensuring the security of transit communications (COMSEC). The TSEC/KW-26 aimed to shield COMSEC operations from exploitation and disruption, focusing on maintaining the integrity and availability of its systems (Klein 1). Through the cryptographic equipment's lifetime, it achieved the transition of encryption from paper-based to electronic hardware, and set standards that would help drive forward automatic software in cybersecurity.

## **2. History of Development**

The implementations of encryption technology surrounding and utilizing the TSEC/KW-26 equipment saw extensive progression throughout the 20th century. Under NSA leadership, including the chief of records and data Howard Barlow, research was launched into Electronic Teletypewritter (TTY) encryption. This type of encryption in the early to late 1910s provided the foundations for transitioning to more automated and electric equipment. In 1952, technicians at NSA furthered their studies by synthesizing a "proof of concept" model that focused on one-time key cards, leading to a more modern hardware (Klein 6). A common fill device (CFD) was utilized, where the insertion of a key card would lock it in place. After use, the card was severed in order to maintain security regulations regarding COMSEC. This marks the start of the NSA's first KW-26 experimental model.

However, development with electronic encryption equipment can be more prodominantly traced to engineer Gilbert S. Vernam. In 1917, he invents a mechanical method for tap-mixing with online encryption. Vernam is able to obtain patents and the technology becomes commercialized by companies like AT&T (Klein 3). It was not until post World War 2 (WW2), that more robust and defined technology would gain traction for widespread use. The success of cipher algorithms in the war prompted the need for cryptographic security for data traffic in the United States.

As a result, manual systems were replaced across the military to allow for more automatic functionality. They followed the one-time key card model, allowing for secure encryption, but also requiring massive physical discarding of cards and tapes. Throughout the second half of the 20th century, continued production called for inital orders of 1,500 units for the navy in 1957 (Klein 10). The KW-26 becomes increasing utilized and deployed, with over 14,000 units in service across the 1960s. Finally, during the mid-1980s, the KW-26 finishes its decades of service and is decommissioned by the NSA. Its successor, the solid state KG-84, takes over and continues improving cryptographic technology for data security (Klein 11).
